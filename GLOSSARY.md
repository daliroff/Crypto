# Crypto Trading Glossary

A reference guide to 80+ essential cryptocurrency and trading terms. Terms are organized alphabetically. Each definition is written to be accurate, plain-language, and practically useful for traders at any level.

---

## A

**Airdrop** — A distribution of free tokens sent directly to wallet addresses, usually as a reward for early adoption, holding a related token, or completing specific on-chain actions. Projects use airdrops for marketing and to bootstrap community ownership. Recipients should verify legitimacy carefully, as fake airdrops are a common scam vector.

**Altcoin** — Any cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin. The term originally referred to "alternative coins" launched after Bitcoin, but it now encompasses thousands of tokens with vastly different use cases, technologies, and risk profiles. Most altcoins are highly correlated to Bitcoin during broad market moves.

**AMM (Automated Market Maker)** — A type of decentralized exchange protocol that uses mathematical formulas — most commonly x * y = k — to price assets and settle trades without a traditional order book. Users trade against a liquidity pool rather than a counterparty. AMMs are the backbone of most DeFi exchanges, including Uniswap and Curve.

**APR (Annual Percentage Rate)** — The annualized rate of return on an investment or cost of a loan, expressed without compounding. In crypto, APR is commonly used when quoting staking or lending rewards. It is a simpler figure than APY and will always be lower when compounding occurs more than once per year.

**APY (Annual Percentage Yield)** — The annualized rate of return that accounts for the effect of compounding interest. If you reinvest your rewards, APY reflects how much you actually earn over a year. Comparing APY across DeFi protocols requires caution — quoted rates can be based on short-term snapshots and may not hold over time.

**Arbitrage** — The practice of simultaneously buying an asset in one market and selling it in another to profit from a price discrepancy. In crypto, arbitrageurs exploit price differences between exchanges, pairs, or blockchain networks. Arbitrage activity helps keep prices consistent across markets, but opportunities are often fleeting and require fast execution.

**ATH (All-Time High)** — The highest price an asset has ever reached since it began trading. ATHs are frequently referenced as psychological resistance levels, since many holders who bought near the top are waiting to break even before selling. A sustained break above a previous ATH is often seen as a bullish signal.

**ATL (All-Time Low)** — The lowest price an asset has ever traded at. ATLs mark the floor of an asset's price history and are sometimes used as reference points for extreme fear or capitulation events.

---

## B

**Bear Market** — A sustained period of declining prices, typically defined as a drop of 20% or more from recent highs, accompanied by pessimistic sentiment. In crypto, bear markets often last 12–24 months and see assets fall 70–90% or more from their peaks. They are also periods when long-term investors accumulate.

**Block** — A bundle of validated transactions that is permanently recorded on the blockchain. Each block contains a cryptographic hash of the previous block, linking them in a chain. New blocks are added at regular intervals — roughly every 10 minutes on Bitcoin and every 12 seconds on Ethereum — depending on the network's consensus rules.

**Breakout** — A price move that pushes through an established support or resistance level, often accompanied by increased volume. Breakouts signal that the prevailing range may be ending and a new trend beginning. Traders look for confirmed breakouts (a close beyond the level) rather than brief wicks to reduce false signals.

**Bull Market** — A sustained period of rising prices and positive market sentiment. In crypto, bull markets are characterized by increasing retail interest, new ATHs, and aggressive risk-taking. They often end in euphoric excess before giving way to a bear cycle.

---

## C

**Candlestick** — A chart element that represents price action over a defined time period. Each candle displays four data points: open, high, low, and close (OHLCV). The body shows the open-to-close range; the wicks (shadows) extend to the high and low. Candlestick patterns are used in technical analysis to identify potential reversals or continuations.

**CEX (Centralized Exchange)** — A cryptocurrency exchange operated by a central company that holds user funds in custodial wallets, matches orders, and facilitates trades. Examples include Binance, Coinbase, and Kraken. CEXs offer ease of use and deep liquidity but require trusting the exchange with your assets — a risk illustrated by collapses like FTX.

**Circulating Supply** — The number of coins or tokens currently in public circulation and available for trading. It excludes coins that are locked, reserved, or not yet issued. Circulating supply is the figure used in the market cap calculation and is the most relevant supply metric for short-term price analysis.

**Coin** — A cryptocurrency that operates on its own native blockchain, such as Bitcoin (BTC) on the Bitcoin network or Ether (ETH) on Ethereum. This distinguishes coins from tokens, which are built on top of an existing blockchain. The distinction matters for understanding the underlying infrastructure and security model.

**Consolidation** — A period of sideways price action following a significant move, where the asset trades within a relatively tight range. Consolidation indicates that buyers and sellers are reaching temporary equilibrium. It often precedes the next directional move, and traders use volume and pattern analysis to anticipate which way the range resolves.

**Correction** — A short-term price decline of 10% or more from a recent peak, occurring within an otherwise ongoing trend. Corrections are healthy and normal — they shake out overleveraged positions and allow new buyers to enter. A correction that deepens and reverses the broader trend is reclassified as the start of a bear market.

## D

**DAO (Decentralized Autonomous Organization)** — An organization governed by rules encoded in smart contracts and controlled by token holders who vote on proposals. DAOs remove traditional hierarchical management, replacing it with on-chain governance. Examples include MakerDAO and Uniswap governance. They enable decentralized coordination but face challenges around voter apathy and legal recognition.

**DCA (Dollar-Cost Averaging)** — An investment strategy where a fixed dollar amount is invested at regular intervals regardless of price. DCA reduces the impact of volatility and removes the pressure of timing the market. Over long periods in rising markets, it typically results in a lower average cost per unit than attempting to buy all at once.

**DeFi (Decentralized Finance)** — A broad ecosystem of financial applications built on blockchains — primarily Ethereum — that operate without intermediaries like banks or brokerages. DeFi protocols enable lending, borrowing, trading, and yield generation through smart contracts. While offering unprecedented access and transparency, DeFi also carries smart contract, liquidity, and regulatory risks.

**DEX (Decentralized Exchange)** — A cryptocurrency exchange that operates through smart contracts on a blockchain, allowing users to trade directly from their own wallets without a central custodian. Trades are settled on-chain. Leading DEXs include Uniswap, Curve, and dYdX. DEXs offer self-custody and censorship resistance but may have lower liquidity and higher fees than CEXs.

**DYOR (Do Your Own Research)** — A common reminder in crypto communities that investors should independently verify information before making decisions rather than relying on social media hype, influencer calls, or anonymous advice. DYOR is both a practical warning and a disclaimer frequently used before sharing opinions about assets.

---

## E

**EMA (Exponential Moving Average)** — A type of moving average that gives greater weight to more recent price data, making it more responsive to current market conditions than the SMA. EMAs are widely used to identify trend direction and dynamic support/resistance. Common EMA periods include 9, 21, 50, and 200.

---

## F

**Flash Loan** — An uncollateralized loan that must be borrowed and repaid within a single blockchain transaction. If the borrower cannot repay by the end of the transaction, the entire operation is reversed and treated as if it never happened. Flash loans are used for arbitrage, collateral swaps, and — notoriously — for exploiting vulnerabilities in DeFi protocols.

**FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)** — The anxiety-driven impulse to buy an asset that has already risen sharply, driven by fear of missing further gains. FOMO leads traders to enter positions at poor prices and without a plan. It is one of the most common and costly emotional biases in trading, often triggering buys near local tops.

**FUD (Fear, Uncertainty, and Doubt)** — Negative information — sometimes false or exaggerated — spread to erode confidence in an asset, project, or market. FUD can be organic (genuine concern) or deliberate (to suppress prices before accumulation). Recognizing the difference between genuine risk analysis and manufactured FUD is a critical skill.

---

## G

**Gas** — The fee paid to compensate network validators or miners for the computational resources required to process and record a transaction on a blockchain like Ethereum. Gas is denominated in Gwei (a fraction of ETH). Gas prices fluctuate with network demand — during high-activity periods, fees can spike dramatically.

**GM** — "Good morning" — a casual greeting used widely in crypto communities on social media and in Discord servers. It signals community participation and positivity. While trivial on the surface, the habit of saying GM is often cited as a cultural identifier of long-term community members.

---

## H

**Hash** — A fixed-length string of characters produced by a cryptographic hash function when applied to any input data. Hashes are deterministic (same input always yields same output) and one-way (you cannot reverse a hash to find the original data). Blockchain uses hashes to link blocks, verify transactions, and secure wallets.

**HODL** — A deliberate misspelling of "hold," originating from a 2013 Bitcoin forum post, now widely used to describe the strategy of holding cryptocurrency long-term regardless of price volatility. It has since been retroactively interpreted as "Hold On for Dear Life." HODLing is often contrasted with active trading.

---

## I

**ICO (Initial Coin Offering)** — A fundraising method in which a project sells newly created tokens to the public in exchange for capital, typically in Bitcoin or ETH. ICOs were extremely popular in 2017 but largely unregulated, leading to widespread fraud and project failures. Regulatory scrutiny has since shifted many projects toward IDOs or private sales.

**IDO (Initial DEX Offering)** — A fundraising model in which a project launches its token directly on a decentralized exchange, allowing public participation without a centralized intermediary. IDOs are generally more accessible and decentralized than ICOs but can still carry high risk due to limited vetting and susceptibility to bot manipulation at launch.

**Impermanent Loss** — The temporary loss in value that liquidity providers experience when the price ratio of assets in an AMM pool changes compared to when they were deposited. If you simply held the assets outside the pool, you might have more value. The loss is "impermanent" because it can reverse if prices return to the original ratio, but it becomes permanent upon withdrawal.

---

## L

**Layer 1 (L1)** — The base blockchain network itself — the foundational protocol where transactions are ultimately settled. Examples include Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Solana. Layer 1 is responsible for security, decentralization, and final settlement but often faces scalability trade-offs.

**Layer 2 (L2)** — A secondary protocol built on top of a Layer 1 blockchain to improve scalability by processing transactions off the main chain and periodically settling the results on L1. Examples include Arbitrum, Optimism, and the Lightning Network. L2s reduce fees and increase throughput while inheriting security from the underlying L1.

**Leverage** — The use of borrowed capital to amplify the size of a trading position beyond what one's own funds would allow. A 10x leveraged position means $1,000 of capital controls $10,000 of exposure. Leverage magnifies both profits and losses and dramatically increases the risk of liquidation — a total loss of the margin posted.

**Limit Order** — An order to buy or sell an asset at a specific price or better, which will only execute if the market reaches that price. Limit orders give traders price control and qualify as maker orders (adding liquidity), often receiving lower fees. The trade-off is that the order may not fill if the price never reaches the specified level.

**Liquidity** — The ease with which an asset can be bought or sold without significantly moving its price. High-liquidity markets have tight bid-ask spreads and large order books. Low liquidity leads to slippage and price manipulation. In DeFi, liquidity refers to assets deposited into pools that enable trading.

---

## M

**MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)** — A momentum indicator that shows the relationship between two exponential moving averages (typically 12-period and 26-period EMA) by plotting their difference (the MACD line), a signal line (9-period EMA of MACD), and a histogram. Crossovers and divergences between the MACD line and signal line generate buy and sell signals.

**Maker** — A trader who places a limit order that is not immediately filled, thereby adding an order to the order book and providing liquidity to the market. Exchanges typically reward maker orders with lower fees than taker orders because they improve market depth. The term comes from "making the market."

**Margin** — The collateral deposited to open and maintain a leveraged trading position. Margin represents the trader's actual capital at risk. If a position moves against the trader and losses approach the margin amount, the exchange issues a margin call or automatically liquidates the position to prevent a negative balance.

**Market Cap (Market Capitalization)** — The total market value of a cryptocurrency, calculated by multiplying the current price by the circulating supply. Market cap is used to rank and compare cryptocurrencies. It does not represent the total capital invested — rather, it reflects what all existing coins would be worth if sold at the current price simultaneously.

**Market Order** — An order to buy or sell an asset immediately at the best available price in the current order book. Market orders guarantee execution but not price — in low-liquidity conditions, significant slippage can occur. Market orders are taker orders and generally carry higher fees.

**Max Supply** — The absolute maximum number of coins or tokens that will ever exist for a given cryptocurrency, as defined by its protocol or smart contract. Bitcoin's max supply is 21 million. Not all assets have a max supply — some are inflationary by design. Max supply is relevant for understanding long-term scarcity.

**Mempool (Memory Pool)** — A holding area on each blockchain node where unconfirmed transactions wait before being selected by miners or validators and included in a block. When network demand is high, the mempool fills up and users must pay higher fees to have their transactions prioritized. Monitoring the mempool helps traders time transactions during low-fee periods.

**Miner** — A participant in a Proof of Work blockchain network who uses computational power to solve cryptographic puzzles and validate new blocks of transactions. Miners are rewarded with newly minted coins (block subsidy) and transaction fees. Mining requires significant energy and hardware investment.

---

## N

**NFT (Non-Fungible Token)** — A unique digital token on a blockchain that represents ownership of a specific item — such as artwork, music, gaming assets, or collectibles — and cannot be exchanged on a one-to-one basis with another token (unlike fungible currencies). NFTs use smart contracts to establish provenance and enable transfers, though they do not inherently prevent copying of the underlying media.

**NFA (Not Financial Advice)** — A disclaimer used when sharing opinions about crypto assets or markets to clarify that the content is informational only and should not be interpreted as professional investment advice. Its use is essentially universal in crypto social media and communities, and it signals the listener to apply their own judgment.

**Node** — A computer that participates in a blockchain network by maintaining a copy of the ledger and verifying transactions according to the network's consensus rules. Full nodes store the entire blockchain history and validate every transaction independently. Running your own node is the most trustless way to interact with a blockchain.

---

## O

**OBV (On-Balance Volume)** — A technical indicator that cumulates volume over time by adding the period's volume when price closes higher and subtracting it when price closes lower. OBV is used to confirm trend direction: rising OBV during a price uptrend suggests buying pressure, while divergence between price and OBV can signal a weakening trend.

**OHLCV** — An acronym for the five data points recorded for each trading period: Open (first trade price), High (highest price), Low (lowest price), Close (last trade price), and Volume (total units traded). OHLCV is the standard data format used in technical analysis and is the input for virtually all chart indicators.

**Order Book** — A real-time list of all open buy (bid) and sell (ask) orders for a trading pair on an exchange, organized by price level. The order book reveals market depth, the current best bid and ask, and where large concentrations of orders exist. Reading the order book is a core skill for understanding short-term supply and demand dynamics.

---

## P

**Private Key** — A secret cryptographic number that allows its holder to authorize transactions and prove ownership of the corresponding wallet address. Anyone with your private key has complete control over your funds. Private keys must never be shared, stored online, or exposed — losing them means permanent loss of access to the wallet.

**Proof of Stake (PoS)** — A consensus mechanism in which validators are chosen to create new blocks and validate transactions based on the amount of cryptocurrency they have staked (locked as collateral). PoS uses far less energy than Proof of Work. Validators who attempt to cheat can have their stake "slashed" (destroyed) as punishment. Ethereum transitioned to PoS in 2022.

**Proof of Work (PoW)** — A consensus mechanism in which miners compete to solve computationally intensive mathematical puzzles to validate transactions and add new blocks. The winner earns the block reward. PoW is highly secure and battle-tested, but it is energy-intensive. Bitcoin is the most prominent PoW network.

**Public Key** — A cryptographic key derived from the private key that serves as your wallet's public-facing identifier. It can be shared freely with others to receive funds. The public key is mathematically linked to the private key, but the private key cannot be derived from the public key — this asymmetry is the foundation of blockchain security.

---

## R

**Resistance** — A price level at which an asset has historically struggled to move above, due to selling pressure from traders who want to exit at that price. Resistance levels are identified on charts and act as potential ceilings for price movement. When price breaks above resistance with conviction, that level often becomes support.

**RSI (Relative Strength Index)** — A momentum oscillator that measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes to evaluate overbought or oversold conditions. RSI ranges from 0 to 100. Readings above 70 are traditionally considered overbought (potential reversal down), and below 30 are considered oversold (potential reversal up). RSI divergences from price are particularly useful signals.

**Rug Pull** — A type of exit scam in which developers or insiders of a crypto project abruptly withdraw all liquidity or dump their token holdings, causing the price to collapse to near zero and leaving other investors with worthless assets. Rug pulls are common in DeFi and with meme tokens. Red flags include anonymous teams, unaudited contracts, and locked liquidity that expires soon.

---

## S

**Seed Phrase (Recovery Phrase / Mnemonic)** — A human-readable backup of a crypto wallet, typically 12 or 24 randomly ordered words. The seed phrase can be used to restore full access to the wallet and all its assets on any compatible software. It must be written down physically and stored securely offline — anyone who obtains it has complete access to the wallet.

**Short Selling** — A strategy of borrowing an asset and selling it at the current price with the intention of buying it back later at a lower price, returning the borrowed asset, and keeping the difference as profit. In crypto, shorting is done through margin trading or derivatives. Short sellers profit from declining prices but face theoretically unlimited loss if the price rises.

**Slippage** — The difference between the expected execution price of a trade and the actual price at which it executes. Slippage occurs because markets move in the time between order submission and fill. It is most pronounced in low-liquidity markets or with large orders. DEXs show estimated slippage before execution and allow traders to set a maximum tolerance.

**SMA (Simple Moving Average)** — An average of an asset's closing prices over a specified number of periods, updated as each new period closes. The SMA gives equal weight to all periods in the window. Common SMAs include 50-day and 200-day, which are watched as major trend indicators. The crossover of shorter over longer SMAs can signal trend changes.

**Smart Contract** — Self-executing code deployed on a blockchain that automatically enforces and executes the terms of an agreement when predefined conditions are met, without requiring an intermediary. Smart contracts are the foundation of DeFi, NFTs, DAOs, and most blockchain applications. Once deployed, they are immutable and execute exactly as written — including any bugs.

**Spread** — The difference between the best available bid (buy) price and the best available ask (sell) price in a market. A tight spread indicates high liquidity and low transaction cost. A wide spread suggests thin liquidity. The spread is effectively an immediate cost borne by market order takers.

**Stablecoin** — A cryptocurrency designed to maintain a stable value, usually pegged to a fiat currency such as the US dollar. Stablecoins can be backed by fiat reserves (USDT, USDC), over-collateralized by crypto (DAI), or maintained through algorithmic mechanisms (historically riskier). They are widely used in DeFi and as a safe haven during market downturns.

**Staking** — The process of locking up cryptocurrency in a Proof of Stake network to participate in validation and earn staking rewards. Stakers act as the network's security layer. The term is also used loosely in DeFi to describe depositing tokens into a protocol in exchange for yield — which carries different risks than native PoS staking.

**Stop Loss** — A pre-set order to sell an asset when it reaches a specified price below the entry point, automatically limiting losses on a trade. Stop losses enforce discipline by removing emotional decision-making when positions move against a trader. Proper stop loss placement — based on chart structure rather than arbitrary dollar amounts — is essential to risk management.

**Support** — A price level at which buying interest has historically been strong enough to prevent the price from falling further. Support levels act as floors in price movement. When price breaks below support convincingly, that level often flips to become resistance. Identifying key support zones is fundamental to both entry planning and stop loss placement.

## T

**Taker** — A trader who places an order that is matched immediately against an existing order in the order book, thereby removing liquidity from the market. Market orders are always taker orders. Exchanges typically charge takers higher fees than makers as an incentive for liquidity providers.

**Take Profit** — A pre-set order to sell an asset when it reaches a specified price above the entry point, locking in gains automatically. Take profit orders ensure that open profits are realized without requiring constant monitoring. They are used alongside stop losses to define a trade's risk-to-reward ratio before entry.

**Token** — A digital asset that is built on top of an existing blockchain rather than having its own native network. Tokens use the underlying blockchain's infrastructure for transaction processing and security. ERC-20 tokens on Ethereum are the most common example. Tokens can represent currencies, utility access, governance rights, or ownership of real-world assets.

**Tokenomics** — The economic design and parameters of a cryptocurrency token, including its total supply, distribution schedule, vesting periods, inflation/deflation mechanisms, utility, and incentive structure. Strong tokenomics align the interests of various stakeholders and support long-term value. Weak tokenomics — such as excessive team allocation or no real utility — are major red flags.

**Total Supply** — The total number of coins or tokens that currently exist, including those in circulation and those that have been minted but are locked or reserved. Total supply differs from circulating supply in that it includes team allocations, treasury reserves, and locked tokens. It represents current existence, not future issuance.

**TVL (Total Value Locked)** — The aggregate value of crypto assets deposited into a DeFi protocol's smart contracts — used as a measure of that protocol's size, adoption, and liquidity. TVL is often cited when comparing DeFi platforms. It can fluctuate dramatically with token prices and is not a perfect indicator of protocol health or security.

---

## V

**Validator** — A participant in a Proof of Stake network who locks up (stakes) cryptocurrency as collateral and is selected to propose or attest to new blocks of transactions. Validators earn staking rewards for honest participation and risk having their stake slashed for malicious behavior or extended downtime. On Ethereum, becoming a solo validator requires 32 ETH.

---

## W

**WAGMI** — "We're All Gonna Make It" — a rallying cry used in crypto and NFT communities to express collective optimism and shared belief in the long-term success of the space. It is often used humorously or sincerely after market downturns as encouragement. Its counterpart, "NGMI" (Not Gonna Make It), is used to describe poor decision-making or excessive pessimism.

**Wallet** — Software or hardware that stores the private keys needed to access and control cryptocurrency holdings on a blockchain. Wallets do not store the coins themselves (those live on the blockchain) — they store the credentials needed to authorize transactions. Wallets range from hardware devices (Ledger, Trezor) to browser extensions (MetaMask) to paper backups.

**Whale** — A holder or trader with a sufficiently large position that their buying or selling activity can meaningfully move the market price of an asset. The threshold varies by asset — a whale in a small-cap token may hold far fewer coins than a whale in Bitcoin. Whale movements are monitored via on-chain analytics to anticipate potential market impact.

**Whitepaper** — A technical and conceptual document published by a crypto project that explains its purpose, technology, token economics, and roadmap. The Bitcoin whitepaper by Satoshi Nakamoto (2008) is the original and most famous example. Evaluating a project's whitepaper is a foundational step in fundamental analysis — a vague or plagiarized whitepaper is a major warning sign.

---

## Y

**Yield Farming** — A DeFi strategy where users move assets between protocols to maximize returns from interest, trading fees, and governance token rewards. Yield farming often involves providing liquidity to AMM pools or lending platforms and compounding rewards across multiple steps. Returns can be high but so are risks — including smart contract exploits, impermanent loss, and reward token devaluation.
